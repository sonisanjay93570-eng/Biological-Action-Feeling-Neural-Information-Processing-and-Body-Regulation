# Biological-Action-Feeling-Neural-Information-Processing-and-Body-Regulation
A Process-Based Conceptual Framework  Author: Sanjay Soni Research Identity: Sanjay Soni Research Research Area: Conceptual Science and Philosophy
Framework: Action-Flow Theory
Research Status: Conceptual framework open to scientific review, mathematical analysis, experimental testing, and independent verification.

1. Abstract

The human body continuously performs biological activities, receives information from the environment, processes internal signals, and regulates its responses through interconnected physiological systems.

The nervous system plays a central role in transmitting and processing information associated with sensation, movement, memory, attention, and bodily regulation. However, biological responses do not always require language, and conscious experience is not identical to the verbal description of that experience.

This conceptual study explores the relationship between biological activity, sensory information, neural processing, feeling, attention, learning, and body regulation.

The central question is whether these processes can be understood within a shared process-based framework in which biological interactions generate signals, neural networks process information, and coordinated responses emerge through interactions between the nervous system and the rest of the body.

The framework does not claim to establish a new biological law. Instead, it organizes established scientific concepts and proposes questions for further investigation.

2. Central Research Question

Can biological actions, sensory experiences, neural information processing, attention, and bodily control be understood as interconnected processes without assuming that language is necessary for every form of experience?

A related question is:

How does the biological system distinguish between receiving information, experiencing a sensation, interpreting its significance, and producing an appropriate response?

3. Biological Activity and Information

The human body is an active biological system.

Its cells continuously perform chemical reactions, exchange substances, maintain electrical gradients, and interact with other cells.

Examples include:

- Muscle contraction and relaxation.
- Transmission of electrical signals by neurons.
- Chemical communication between cells.
- Regulation of body temperature.
- Changes in heart rate and breathing.
- Sensory responses to touch, pressure, heat, and pain.

These activities do not all depend on conscious decisions.

Many physiological processes occur automatically through interactions among the nervous system, endocrine system, immune system, and other regulatory mechanisms.

Therefore, biological activity must be distinguished from conscious awareness of that activity.

4. Sensory Information and Neural Signalling

The body contains specialized sensory receptors that respond to particular forms of stimulation.

For example:

- Photoreceptors respond to light.
- Mechanoreceptors respond to mechanical stimulation.
- Thermoreceptors respond to temperature.
- Nociceptors respond to potentially damaging stimuli.

These receptors convert suitable forms of stimulation into biological signals through processes known as sensory transduction.

Neural pathways transmit information to different parts of the nervous system, where it can be processed and integrated.

The resulting activity can contribute to sensation, perception, reflexes, learning, and behavioural responses.

However, not all information follows the same pathway, and not every neural signal necessarily produces a conscious experience.

5. Feeling and Experience Without Language

An important distinction exists between experiencing something and knowing the word used to describe it.

For example, a child who has not yet learned a language may respond to pain, feel warmth, recognize familiar people, or become frightened.

These examples demonstrate that at least some forms of sensation, emotion, learning, and recognition can occur without the ability to describe them verbally.

Language provides an important means of naming experiences, communicating them to others, and reflecting on their meaning.

Nevertheless, language is not a prerequisite for every form of sensory experience.

For this reason, the following processes should be distinguished:

1. Biological stimulation.
2. Sensory transduction.
3. Neural information processing.
4. Sensation or conscious experience.
5. Interpretation and recognition.
6. Verbal description of the experience.

These processes can interact, but they are not identical.

Their precise relationships vary according to the type of sensation, the organism, and the biological circumstances.

6. Feeling, Attention, and Concentration

Feeling can influence attention, while attention can influence how information is processed.

For example, a sudden sound may attract attention automatically. Pain may redirect attention toward an injured area. A familiar activity may receive less conscious attention after repeated practice.

Concentration involves maintaining or directing attention toward a particular task, stimulus, or mental activity.

It depends on interactions among multiple neural systems, including systems associated with attention, memory, motivation, and executive control.

A feeling may therefore influence concentration, but feeling alone does not necessarily produce concentration.

Likewise, concentration can be influenced by deliberate effort, environmental conditions, sleep, emotional state, and physiological factors.

Research Question

How do sensory signals, emotional responses, prior learning, and neural activity interact to direct attention without requiring a verbal description of every experience?

This is a research question rather than an established conclusion of the proposed framework.

7. Neural Processing and Body Regulation

The nervous system helps regulate bodily activity through electrical and chemical signalling.

The brain and spinal cord coordinate many movements and responses, while peripheral neural pathways connect the central nervous system with sensory receptors, muscles, organs, and glands.

Some responses are relatively rapid and automatic.

For example, withdrawing a hand from a potentially harmful stimulus may involve a spinal reflex. The response can begin before the person consciously evaluates what happened.

Other responses involve more extensive processing by the brain, including conscious decisions, learned behaviour, and voluntary movement.

The autonomic nervous system also participates in regulating functions such as heart rate, digestion, and aspects of breathing.

However, bodily control is not performed by the brain alone. It emerges through interactions among neural, hormonal, muscular, and other physiological mechanisms.

Consequently, the proposed framework treats body regulation as a coordinated biological process rather than the action of a single independent controller.

8. A Process-Based Model

Within Action-Flow Theory, the relationships discussed above can be represented through the following conceptual sequence:

Environmental or Internal Condition

↓

Biological Interaction

↓

Sensory Detection and Signal Generation

↓

Neural Transmission and Processing

↓

Possible Sensation, Perception, or Emotional Response

↓

Attention, Interpretation, or Learning

↓

Possible Behavioural or Physiological Response

↓

New Biological Condition

↓

Further Interaction and Feedback

This sequence is a simplified conceptual representation, not a universal pathway followed by every biological process.

Some responses occur without conscious perception. Others involve feedback loops, parallel processing, or interactions that do not follow a simple linear order.

The model therefore allows for interconnected and recurring processes rather than requiring a single fixed sequence.

9. Action, Reaction, and Result

The framework distinguishes three related concepts.

9.1 Action

An action is an event or process that changes a biological or physical condition.

9.2 Reaction

A reaction is a response produced through interactions within a system.

The response may be automatic, reflexive, learned, or deliberately initiated.

9.3 Result

A result is the resulting condition or change associated with a process.

Importantly, a result can occur without being consciously recognized.

For example, body temperature can change without a person noticing it. A reflex can occur without deliberate thought. A person may also experience a sensation without knowing its scientific explanation.

Thus, the existence of a result and the recognition of that result are different matters.

Proposed conceptual distinction:

Biological processes can produce results, while sensory and cognitive processes can contribute to detecting, experiencing, interpreting, and communicating those results.

This distinction is compatible with established scientific knowledge and can serve as a basis for further conceptual analysis.

10. Is Experience a Biological Process?

The framework proposes examining experience in relation to biological activity and neural information processing.

Scientific research establishes that conscious perception is closely associated with brain activity. Sensory systems, neural networks, memory, attention, and other processes contribute to how organisms respond to and interpret their surroundings.

However, a complete explanation of how subjective experience arises from physical processes remains an active area of scientific and philosophical investigation.

Therefore, this study does not claim that the nature of consciousness has been fully explained.

Instead, it asks whether a process-based analysis can help distinguish the physical mechanisms associated with sensation from the subjective character of experience.

The distinction is particularly important because measuring a neural signal is not the same as directly measuring everything a person experiences.

11. Established Knowledge and the Proposed Framework

The following distinctions are essential for responsible scientific communication.

Established scientific knowledge includes:

- Sensory receptors detect particular forms of stimulation.
- Neurons transmit information through electrical and chemical signalling.
- Neural systems participate in sensation, perception, attention, movement, and learning.
- Bodily functions are regulated through interacting physiological systems.
- Some biological responses occur without language or conscious deliberation.
- A physiological result can occur without being consciously recognized.

The proposed conceptual contribution is:

- Organizing these processes within a common action-and-response framework.
- Distinguishing biological events from the experience and interpretation of those events.
- Examining how feeling, attention, information processing, and bodily regulation interact.
- Investigating whether a process-based framework can generate clearly defined, testable research questions.

The proposed framework does not establish a new biological mechanism merely by connecting these concepts.

Its scientific value will depend on whether it produces clearer explanations, precise predictions, measurable variables, or useful results beyond existing models.

12. Questions for Future Research

The following questions may guide further investigation:

1. How do different sensory signals influence attention and behavioural responses?
2. Which neural processes distinguish automatic responses from responses involving conscious perception?
3. How does language change the recognition, memory, and communication of experiences?
4. How do emotion and attention influence one another?
5. What measurable relationships exist between neural activity and reported subjective experience?
6. How do neural and hormonal systems coordinate the regulation of bodily functions?
7. Can a process-based model predict any aspect of these relationships more accurately than an existing model?

Answering these questions would require appropriate scientific literature, clearly defined terms, validated measurement methods, and independent evaluation.

Human research would also require suitable ethical safeguards.

13. Conclusion

The human body performs biological activities, receives sensory information, processes neural signals, and regulates its responses through interconnected systems.

Feeling, attention, interpretation, and language are related to these processes but should not be treated as identical.

A person can experience certain sensations without knowing the words used to describe them. A biological response can occur without conscious recognition, and a result can exist before a person understands its significance.

Within Action-Flow Theory, these relationships are considered through a process-based conceptual framework that connects biological activity, information processing, experience, and regulation.

The framework remains open to criticism, scientific correction, mathematical development where appropriate, and independent verification.

Its purpose is not to replace established neuroscience or to present an untested hypothesis as a proven scientific discovery. Its purpose is to formulate a clear conceptual question:

How are biological activity, sensory information, subjective experience, and bodily regulation connected, and what can scientific investigation establish about their relationships?
